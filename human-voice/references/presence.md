# Presence: someone is there

A draft can pass every rule in SKILL.md and still read as AI. Nothing in it is wrong. What's missing is a person.
Readers notice that before they can name it. AI writing reads as if it came from nowhere, about no one in
particular, and was finished with equal care in every line. Human writing comes from one place, at one time,
and is uneven: it spends words on what the writer cares about and rushes past the rest.

## What the evidence says

- **People who use LLMs a lot can spot them.** In Russell, Karpinska and Iyyer (ACL 2025), five such readers
  voting together misjudged 1 article in 300, even after the AI text was "humanized". Vocabulary was the top
  clue (53% of explanations), then sentence structure (36%) and originality (24%). The human clues they cited
  include short choppy sentences, filler words such as "just" and "really", varied formatting, and an opening
  that starts with a scene rather than the main point. A positive, inspirational tone gave away even the
  humanized texts.
- **Surface tricks don't make writing human.** Jakesch, Hancock and Naaman (PNAS 2023) found that people read
  first-person pronouns, contractions and family topics as human cues, and that those cues are easy to fake. Adding
  "I" and "don't" is not the fix.
- **What professional writers cut from LLM prose.** Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu (CHI 2025) had professional writers
  edit LLM paragraphs, 8,035 edits in all. Awkward word choice made up 28%, poor sentence structure 20%,
  unnecessary exposition 18% and cliché 17%. The other categories were purple prose, lack of specific detail, and
  tense slips. The paper notes LLMs spell out the subtext ("telling instead of showing"). It also cites Tian et
  al. (2024), who found LLM stories "homogeneously positive".
- **AI text converges.** Several 2024-25 studies found that LLM outputs share ideas and plot shapes across runs
  and across models, even when the words differ. The default angle is the one the reader has already met.
- **The useless true detail.** Barthes ("The Reality Effect", 1968) pointed at Flaubert's barometer, a detail that
  does no work for the plot and whose only message is "this was real". Tom Wolfe built his nonfiction on "status
  details" gathered by staying with people long enough to see the revealing moments.
- **Anecdote, then reflection.** Ira Glass describes a story as a sequence of actions (the anecdote, which pulls
  the reader along by raising questions) followed by a moment of reflection that says why it matters. AI writing
  skips the anecdote and serves only reflection.
- **Start late.** Vonnegut's fifth rule: "Start as close to the end as possible."

## The six signs of a person

1. **A witness.** The writer saw something: a time, a place, a person, what was said. "Last March a buyer in
   Hamburg rejected 4,000 jackets" carries a person. "Buyers may reject shipments" carries no one. If the writer
   witnessed nothing (both examples here are invented, to show the form), the piece needs a real moment from the source or from the user. Never invent one (see "The
   witness rule" below).
2. **Uneven attention.** One part gets three paragraphs because the writer cares. Another gets one line. AI gives
   every section the same length, the same care and the same tidy ending. Let one section be short and plain.
   Let a paragraph end on a fact, not a line.
3. **Trust in the reader.** People stop once the point is made. AI explains the point, then explains why it
   matters, then sums it up. Cut every sentence that tells the reader what the previous sentence showed: "That's
   what makes...", "This is why...", "In other words...". Cut the sentences that announce reasoning ("Here is the
   straight answer") and the sentences that perform empathy ("It's a fair question"). If the empathy is real, act
   on it: answer the question.
4. **Skin in the game.** A person has something to lose. They admit what's inconvenient for them ("we sell this
   test, so weigh our view accordingly"), what they got wrong, what they still don't know, or what they dislike.
   AI is homogeneously positive and never uncomfortable. One honest admission is worth more than three reassurances.
5. **A true detail that does no work.** One detail that isn't needed for the argument, only true. The smell of
   the burnt sample, the time on the email, the colour of the tag. It says "someone was there". It must be true
   and from the source or the user. A made-up "useless detail" is the purest slop.
6. **Talk texture.** Real writing carries some spoken grain: a fragment, an aside in brackets, "just", "honestly",
   a sentence that starts with "And" or "But", a word the writer clearly enjoys. Use it sparingly. Grain sprinkled
   on a hollow piece is the Jakesch trap. It only works on a piece that already has a witness.

## The witness rule

Before drafting anything longer than a chat reply, find **one real moment**: something that happened, to someone,
at a time, with a detail. Look in this order:

1. **The source.** Read it for events, not claims. A date, a named case, a thing someone said.
2. **The user.** If the source has none, ask the user one question before drafting: "What's one time this
   actually happened: who, when, what did they say?" One question, cheap to answer. Never more than one.
3. **The reader's own moment.** If you can't ask, open on a moment the reader has certainly lived, in the
   second person and in the present tense: "You're signing off the packing list when the buyer's email lands."
   Keep it to what's universally true of their job. Don't add invented specifics (no names, no numbers).
4. **An honest position.** If none of these exist, say where you stand: "We test this every week; we've never
   worked a cutting floor." A clear position is a kind of presence.

Never invent an anecdote, a customer, a quote, a number or a memory, and never put "I remember" in a writer's
mouth. A fake witness is worse than none.

## Structure: scene, then point

Argument-shaped writing (claim, explanation, closing line, repeated) is the AI default. Story-shaped writing
moves:

1. **Open on the moment** (the anecdote): what happened, in plain past or present tense, with one true detail.
   Start as late as possible, at the point where something goes wrong or someone decides.
2. **Let the reader ask the question.** Don't ask it for them. The moment raises it.
3. **Then the point**, briefly. This is the reflection: what the moment means for this reader.
4. **Then the useful part** (how, what it costs, what to do), plain and short.
5. **Stop.** Not on an aphorism. A next step, a plain fact, or a callback to the moment.

The "point first" rule still holds for emails, replies, summaries and notes. For blogs, articles, newsletters and
posts, the point can come in the second paragraph if the first paragraph is a real moment that sets it up. The
headline or the bold line still carries the point for scanners.

## The roughen pass

Run it after the lint, on the whole draft, before anything else:

- **Count the polished lines.** A polished line is a quotable, aphoristic sentence, usually a closer ("Nobody can
  see a molecule", "A fault you can't see is still a fault"). Keep one per piece, two at most in a long one. Turn
  the rest into plain statements or cut them. A writer who coins a line at the end of every section is performing.
- **Break the section pattern.** If every section has the same shape (claim, explain, button), change two of
  them. Make one shorter. Let one end mid-thought on a fact.
- **Delete the bridges.** Remove transitions that only connect ("That's why", "And so", "This is where"). If the
  next paragraph still follows, the bridge was padding.
- **Find the one place you care most** and give it more room. Shrink the rest.
- **Check the temperature.** Is everything positive and resolved? Leave one thing open or uncomfortable if it's
  true.
- **Read it aloud, or imagine saying it across a table.** Change any sentence you wouldn't say to someone's face.

## Self-test: the dinner-table question

Picture the writer telling this to one person at dinner. Would they open like this? Would they say this sentence
out loud? Would they stop before the summary? If the answer is no three times, it still reads as AI.
