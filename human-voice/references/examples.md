# Examples: before and after

Eleven forms, each with a typical AI draft ("Before") and a rewrite in this skill's voice ("After"). The names,
companies and numbers are invented for illustration. The bread science in example 5 is real.
Read 2 or 3 examples close to your task, then write your own. Don't copy the lines: copy the moves.

---

## 1. Blog post (opening and two sections, wit dial 2)

**Before**
> In today's fast-paced business landscape, meetings have become an essential part of how teams collaborate.
> However, it's important to note that not all meetings are created equal. In this post, we'll delve into
> strategies to make your meetings more productive, efficient, and engaging.

**After**
```text
# Your weekly meeting is a status report wearing a suit

Our Monday meeting had eight people in it and ran for 75 minutes. It produced two decisions. I did the maths one week: that's ten hours of senior time per decision, every week, forever.

**Most recurring meetings aren't for deciding things. They're for reporting things, and reporting is what writing is for.**

So we tried an experiment. Everyone posts their update in a shared doc by Friday. On Monday we discuss only the items someone has flagged as needing a decision. If nothing is flagged, the meeting is cancelled and everyone gets the hour back.

## Status belongs in writing

A written update takes three minutes to read, and anyone it doesn't concern can skip it. Spoken aloud, the same update costs everyone in the room those three minutes, plus the five they spent waiting for their turn.

## The catch

For some teams, the status round is the only time they see each other. Kill it and you lose the small talk that tells you who's struggling. We kept a 15-minute Thursday call with no agenda at all. It's optional, and nearly everyone comes.

Six weeks in, Monday's meeting runs 25 minutes when it happens. It's been cancelled twice. Nobody has asked for it back.

Try it on one meeting this week. If anyone notices it's gone, you'll find out exactly what it was for.
```

**Why it works**
- The title is a claim with a picture in it, not a topic ("Meeting best practices").
- The opening is a real number with tension (75 minutes, two decisions), and the maths makes the point for you.
- The point arrives in paragraph 2, in bold.
- The headings are claims. "The catch" is the turn: it admits what the advice costs and how they fixed it.
- The close is a small, specific action, plus a dry payoff.

## 2. Email with bad news, to a client (wit dial 1)

**Before**
> Subject: Project Update
> Hi Sarah, I hope this email finds you well. I wanted to reach out to provide an update on the project timeline.
> Due to unforeseen circumstances, we have encountered some challenges that may potentially impact our delivery
> date. We are working diligently to resolve these issues and will keep you informed.

**After**
```text
Subject: Launch moves from 3 March to 14 March: two blockers, both fixable

Hi Sarah,

The launch will slip by 11 days, to 14 March. I'd rather tell you now than on 2 March.

Two things caused it:
- **Payment provider review:** their compliance check takes 10 working days, not the 3 we planned for. We submitted on 18 February.
- **Arabic layout:** right-to-left text breaks the checkout page on small phones. We know the fix; it takes about four days.

Nothing else is at risk, and the budget doesn't change.

I need one decision from you by Friday. We can launch English-only on 3 March and add Arabic on the 14th, or launch both together on the 14th. I'd split it, because your March campaign starts on the 5th.

Ali
```

**Why it works**
- The subject line alone tells Sarah the news.
- The first line is the news. The second explains the timing of the email, which builds trust.
- "Unforeseen circumstances" becomes two named causes with dates.
- It ends with a decision she can make, a deadline and a recommendation. There's no "let me know if you have questions".

## 3. Chat reply to a colleague (wit dial 2)

**Context:** "Is it safe to deploy the billing change this Friday afternoon?"

**Before**
> Great question! There are several factors to consider when deploying on a Friday. While the change itself appears
> relatively low-risk, it's important to note that weekend support coverage may be limited. Ultimately, it depends
> on your team's risk tolerance. Let me know if you'd like to discuss further!

**After**
```text
The code's fine; the timing isn't. Tests pass and the change is small, but if invoices go out wrong, nobody's around until Monday and billing errors pile up overnight. Ship it Monday at 10 and I'll watch the dashboard with you. Friday-afternoon deploys are how weekends become stories.
```

**Why it works**
- The answer comes in the first six words, and it's a real answer, not "it depends".
- The one reason given is specific (invoices, overnight).
- It offers a concrete alternative and some help.
- One joke, at the very end.

## 4. LinkedIn post (wit dial 2)

**Before**
> 🚀 I'm thrilled to announce that after an incredible journey, our team has launched our new onboarding experience!
> 🎉 This game-changing initiative empowers users to unlock their full potential. Huge thanks to the amazing team!
> #Innovation #Growth #Leadership #TeamWork #ProductManagement

**After**
```text
We cut our signup form from 14 fields to 3. Completed signups went up 38% in a month.

The interesting part is why there were 14 in the first place. Eleven of them existed because someone, years ago, wanted a report that nobody reads now.

What we did:
- Asked every team which fields they'd used in the last 90 days. Three teams answered. Two fields survived.
- Moved everything else to after signup, when people have a reason to answer.
- Kept one optional question: "What made you sign up today?" It's the most useful data we collect.

The catch: sales lost the company-size field they used to route leads, and for two weeks they hated us. We now get it from a data provider for about two pence a lookup.

What's on your forms that nobody has looked at since it was added?
```

**Why it works**
- The first line is a result with two numbers. That earns the "see more" click.
- The lesson is something the reader can apply to their own forms.
- The catch makes the story believable and a little funny.
- It closes with a genuine question, and has no hashtags.

## 5. Explainer for non-experts (wit dial 2)

**Before**
> Have you ever wondered why bread goes stale? Bread staleness is a fascinating phenomenon that involves complex
> chemical processes. In this article, we'll delve into the science behind it.

**After**
```text
**Stale bread isn't dry bread.** A loaf sealed in a plastic bag goes stale almost as fast as one left out, and it has hardly lost any water.

What changes is the starch. Baking swells the starch with water and makes it soft and loose. As the loaf cools, those starch molecules slowly line back up into tight crystals, and the crumb turns firm and crumbly. Food scientists call it retrogradation. You call it Tuesday's sandwich.

Two consequences are useful:
- **The fridge is the worst place for bread.** The crystals form fastest at fridge temperature, so a chilled loaf stales much faster than one on the counter. The freezer almost stops the process.
- **Heat undoes it, briefly.** Warm a stale slice in the oven or toaster and the crystals melt, so the bread softens again. It re-stales quickly as it cools, so eat it warm.

So: the counter for a day or two, the freezer for anything longer, and never the fridge. Your toaster is a time machine with a short battery.
```

**Why it works**
- It opens by correcting what the reader already believes. That's the strongest hook an explainer has.
- It teaches exactly one technical term, and makes a joke about it straight away.
- The bullets turn the science into decisions the reader can make.
- The close restates the advice in plain words, then lands one image.

## 6. Meeting summary (wit dial 2)

**After** (no "Before": the usual AI version is a list of everything that was said)
```text
**Decision: dark mode ships in the June release, on web only. Mobile waits until Q3.**

The room wanted both platforms. Then the mobile team showed that their colours are hard-coded in 140 places, and fixing that first is three weeks of work nobody had budgeted for.

- **Why now:** it's been the most-requested feature for three quarters running (212 requests), and two enterprise renewals mention it.
- **Scope:** a dark theme plus a "match system" setting. No custom themes.
- **Owner:** Priya for web, with design files from Tom by 8 May.

Still open: do charts get their own dark palette, or just inverted colours? Tom will bring two options on Monday.

Best line of the meeting, from Tom: "Our users have been asking for this since before some of us worked here."
```

**Why it works**
- The decision is first, and bold. Anyone who missed the meeting needs only line 1.
- The turn explains why the room didn't get what it wanted, with a number.
- The bullets hold owners, dates and counts, not discussion.
- It closes with a real quote.

## 7. Reply to an angry customer (wit dial 0)

**Before**
> We sincerely apologize for any inconvenience this may have caused. We understand your frustration and take this
> matter very seriously. Our team is looking into it and will get back to you as soon as possible.

**After**
```text
You were charged £96 twice on 2 April, and our support bot sent you round in a loop instead of fixing it. Both of those are our fault.

I've refunded the second charge today. It will show in your bank within 3 to 5 working days, and you'll get a receipt from us by email within the hour.

The double charge came from a bug in our payment retries that affected 41 customers that morning. It's fixed, and all of them have been refunded.

If the refund hasn't appeared by 10 April, reply to this email and it comes straight to me.

Hana, Customer Support Lead
```

**Why it works**
- It names the exact problem in the customer's terms, which shows someone actually read their complaint.
- It owns the fault in one sentence, without "any inconvenience".
- The fix comes with dates. The cause comes with a number, so they know it wasn't just them.
- There's a named human and a direct line. No jokes.

## 8. Release notes

**Before**
> We're excited to announce version 4.2, packed with powerful new features and improvements that will elevate your
> experience! We've also made various bug fixes and performance enhancements.

**After**
```text
**4.2: export anything, and faster search**

**Breaking:** API keys created before 2024 stop working on 1 July. Create a new one under Settings > API; it takes a minute.

New
- Export any report to PDF or CSV from the report menu.
- Search now finds text inside attached files, not only in titles.

Fixed
- Charts no longer go blank after you refresh the page.
- Search is about 3x faster in workspaces with over 10,000 documents.
- The Save button no longer hides behind the keyboard on small phones.
```

**Why it works**
- The breaking change comes first, with the exact step to fix it.
- Features are described as things the user can now do.
- Fixes are described as the symptom the user saw, so people recognise their own bug.

## 9. Feedback on a colleague's draft (wit dial 1)

**Before**
> Great job on this! Overall it's a really solid draft. A few minor suggestions: maybe consider tightening some
> sections and perhaps making the intro a bit more engaging. Let me know your thoughts!

**After**
```text
The strongest thing in it is the store manager's quote on page 2: "we stopped checking the spreadsheet because the spreadsheet was always wrong." It does more work than the whole first page. Move it to the top.

The one change I'd make: the intro spends 300 words on market background before it says what we recommend. Lead with the recommendation, something like: "We should replace the weekly inventory spreadsheet with live stock counts from the tills. It would save each store manager about four hours a week and end the Monday reorder mistakes."

Two small things: the cost table needs a total row, and "synergies" on page 3 can go.

It's persuasive once it gets going. Flip the opening and I'd send it to the board as it is.
```

**Why it works**
- The praise is specific enough to act on ("keep this, move it").
- There's one main change, with a rewritten example, so they don't have to guess what "more engaging" means.
- The small notes are capped at two.
- The overall verdict is honest and ends with a clear next step.

## 10. Bio or about page (wit dial 1-2)

**Before**
> Passionate, results-driven professional with a proven track record of delivering innovative solutions. I thrive
> in dynamic environments and love leveraging cutting-edge technology to empower teams and drive growth.

**After**
```text
I help small manufacturers stop losing orders to spreadsheets. Over six years I've moved about 30 factories from email-and-Excel ordering to systems their staff actually use, usually in under three months.

Before this I ran the night shift at a bottling plant. That's where I learned that any process that needs a manual is a process people will skip at 3am.

If your team spends Monday mornings reconciling orders, email me. The first call is free, and it's usually useful.
```

**Why it works**
- It says what you do and for whom in the first sentence, with no adjectives about yourself.
- One proof, with a number.
- One true, specific human detail, which doubles as the author's philosophy.
- The call to action names the reader's own pain.

## 11. Incident note or apology to users (wit dial 0)

**After** (the usual AI version: "We experienced a brief service disruption and apologise for any inconvenience")
```text
**Yesterday, 14 May, the app was down for 2 hours and 10 minutes, from 09:40 to 11:50 UK time.** You couldn't log in, and changes saved in the few minutes before 09:40 might not have synced.

The cause was ours. A routine database update locked a table that every login needs. Rolling it back took far longer than it should have, because the rollback script had never been tested on a database our size.

What we've done:
- Restored everything. No data was lost: unsynced changes were recovered from local drafts and are back in your account.
- Tested the rollback on a full-size copy of the database. It now takes 4 minutes.
- Moved database updates to 02:00, when usage is lowest.

If anything still looks wrong in your account, reply to this email and our team will check it today.

We know you plan your mornings around this tool. We're sorry we broke one.
```

**Why it works**
- The exact time window goes first, in bold. "Brief disruption" was 130 minutes, and readers know it.
- It owns the cause plainly, including the embarrassing part (the untested rollback).
- Each fix is concrete and checkable.
- The last line is human without being cute. It's dial 0: warmth, but no wit.

## 12. Internal newsletter item, humor done right (wit dial 3)

**Before**
> Exciting news! We are thrilled to announce that our new expense tool, Tally, is now live. Tally serves as a
> streamlined solution for submitting expenses, ensuring a seamless experience for all team members. We believe it
> will play a pivotal role in our finance processes. 🎉

**After**
```text
**From Monday, expenses go through Tally, and a claim takes about two minutes.**

Last quarter the average claim took 11 days and three emails to get paid. One claim for a £4 coffee went through five people. The coffee was cold before the first one replied.

Tally fixes the boring parts. Snap the receipt on your phone, pick the project, press send. Your manager approves it from the notification, and the money arrives in the next Friday pay run. Finance tested it on taxis, hotel bills and Priya's receipt from a Lisbon café that was printed entirely in Portuguese. All three worked.

The old form closes on 31 May. Anything you submit before then still gets paid the old way, just more slowly.

One request: please photograph the receipt, not the coffee.
```

**Why it works**
- The point is first and bold, with the number a reader cares about.
- The jokes are built from true details: the 11 days, the £4 claim, the cold coffee. Each one makes the
  problem vivid; none is decoration.
- The broken list sets a pattern (taxis, hotel bills) and the third item breaks it, with its punchword last.
  Priya is in on the joke, because it's about her receipt, not her.
- The middle stays plain: steps, date, what happens to old claims. Readers trust the facts because the jokes
  stay out of them.
- The closer calls back to the coffee, once, and doubles as a real instruction.
