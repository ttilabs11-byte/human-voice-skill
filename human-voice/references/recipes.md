# Recipes: one shape per form

Lengths are defaults, not quotas. Cut until removing anything more would lose a fact the reader needs.

## Blog post (600-1,200 words)
```
Opening: a specific scene, number or claim that creates a question. 2-4 sentences, no definitions.
The point, stated plainly by paragraph 2 (bold it if the post is skimmable).
3-5 sections. Each heading is a claim, not a topic ("Most meetings are status reports", not "Meeting culture").
  Each section: claim, evidence (a specific), what to do about it.
The turn: where this advice fails, or what it costs.
Close: one concrete action the reader can take this week, or a callback to the opening scene.
```
Write for the scanner as well as the reader. On a typical web page, people read at most about 28% of the words
and more likely 20% (Nielsen Norman Group). They read the title and the opening, then run down the left edge.
So front-load: put the information-carrying word first in each heading ("Shade must be grade 4 or better", not
"Product conformity checks"), and start each paragraph with its point. A reader who only sees the headings and
the first words of each paragraph should still get the argument.

## Article or explainer (800-2,000 words, wit dial 1-2)
```
Lede: the most surprising true thing, in one sentence.
Nut graf: why it matters to this reader, and what they'll understand by the end.
Body: build understanding in the order the reader's questions arrive. One analogy at most, and say where it breaks.
Counterpoint: the strongest objection, stated fairly.
Close: the implication, not a summary.
```

## Email (50-200 words)
```
Subject: the ask or the news, specific ("Launch moves to 14 March: two blockers", not "Update").
Line 1: the ask or the news.
Why, in 1-3 sentences, with the specifics.
What you need from them, and by when.
Sign-off: one line, no "hope this helps".
```
Make one ask. If you truly need two, number them and say which one matters most: readers who see several
requests tend to answer the easiest one or put the whole email off. Make the reply cheap ("Reply A or B is
enough"). Shorter gets answered more: in Rogers and Lasky-Fink's field test with school board members, a 49-word
email drew 4.8% responses and a 127-word version drew 2.7%.

## Bad news, apology or incident note (wit dial 0)
```
What happened, plainly, in the first sentence. Own it ("we", "I").
Impact on them, specifically.
What's fixed now, and what you're doing so it doesn't repeat.
What they need to do, if anything. Then stop. No humour, no "we take this very seriously".
```

## Chat reply (Slack, Teams, comments: 1-80 words)
```
Answer first ("Yes, safe to deploy").
The one reason or caveat.
The link or command, if there is one.
```

## LinkedIn or social post (80-250 words)
```
Line 1: a specific moment or result that makes people click "see more". No "I'm thrilled to announce".
The lesson, in one plain sentence.
2-4 lines of specifics: what happened, what changed, a number.
The turn: what you got wrong or what it cost.
Close: a question you actually want answered, or a single line. At most two hashtags, or none.
```

## Summary (thread, meeting, document, research: 120-250 words)
```
Optional hook of 8 words or fewer that captures the mood.
**Bold verdict**: what the group concluded, or the split itself.
The turn: the caveat or counter-camp, sized honestly.
2-5 bullets with bold labels that are mini-claims. Each holds facts: numbers, owners, dates, commands.
Minority view, in one line.
Close: the decision, the next step, or the best real quote.
```

## Meeting notes (150-300 words)
```
**Decisions** first (bold each one), then **Actions** (owner, task, date), then **Open questions**.
Discussion only where it explains a decision. No "a productive discussion was had".
```

## Release notes (per item, 1-3 lines)
```
Lead with what the user can now do, not what you built ("Export to PDF from any report").
Fixes: the symptom the user saw ("Charts no longer go blank after a refresh").
Breaking changes go first, in bold, with the migration step.
```

## Feedback on someone's work (100-300 words)
```
The one thing that works best, specifically (so they keep it).
The one change that would matter most, with why and an example rewrite.
At most two smaller notes.
Close: your overall read, honestly.
```

## Bio or about page (50-150 words)
```
What you do, for whom, in one plain sentence.
One proof (a number, a client type, a result).
One human detail that is true and specific.
How to reach you, or what to do next.
```

## Reply to an angry customer or critic (60-180 words, wit dial 0)
```
Acknowledge the specific problem in their words, not "your frustration".
Say what's true: the fault, or the misunderstanding, without defending.
What you've done or will do, and when.
A direct line to a human, if one exists.
```

## Explaining to a non-expert (any length, wit dial 1-2)
```
Start from what they already know.
One concrete example before any abstraction.
Name the one term they'll need, and only that one.
End with what this means for them.
```
Watch for the curse of knowledge: the more you know, the more steps you've stopped seeing (Pinker, "The Sense of
Style", ch. 3). Before sending, list the steps between what they know and your point, and put back the one they'd
trip on. Point their gaze at something they can picture (a fridge, a ski lift), not at a concept.
